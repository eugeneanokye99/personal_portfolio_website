Mentoring junior developers is one of the parts of this job I enjoy most, and one I was worse at than I realized when I started doing it seriously. A few things I've learned along the way.

## Answering the question isn't the same as helping

My first instinct used to be to just fix the bug myself and explain what I did. That helps for exactly one bug. Now I try to ask what they've already tried and where they got stuck, and let them arrive at the fix with a nudge rather than a solution. It's slower in the moment and faster over the next six months.

## Code review is a teaching tool, not a gate

A review that only says "change this" trains someone to comply, not to understand. I try to explain the why behind a requested change, even when it takes an extra sentence: not just "extract this into a method" but "this method is doing three unrelated things, and testing them separately will get easier if they're separated first." The extra sentence is the actual mentoring.

## Psychological safety matters more than technical correctness

A junior developer who's afraid to say "I don't understand this" will ship code they don't understand, which is far more dangerous than admitting confusion in standup. The most useful thing I can do as a mentor is make it obviously safe to say "I'm stuck" out loud, early, before it becomes a bigger problem.

## They notice what you do, not what you say

Advising someone to write tests while skipping tests myself under deadline pressure taught me nothing except that I don't practice what I preach. Mentoring, more than most parts of engineering, is led by example.
